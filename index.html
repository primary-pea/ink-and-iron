The problem

A known remedy, a missed target.

Anaemia in women is one of the oldest nutrition targets in global health, and one of the least met.

30%of women aged 15 to 49 worldwide were anaemic in 2019. In 2023, half or more in Gabon, Mali, Mauritania, Côte d'Ivoire and Benin.
2012 → 2030The World Health Assembly set out to halve anaemia in women by 2025, then moved the date to 2030. No sub-Saharan country is on track.
Under 15%Preconception iron and folic acid uptake in several sub-Saharan countries. The remedy is known and cheap. The gap is delivery.

WHO anaemia fact sheet; Stevens et al., Lancet Global Health 2022 (anaemia 2000–2019); WHO/UNICEF progress review toward the 2025 target; Lancet Haematology 2021 review of iron deficiency anaemia in sub-Saharan Africa.

Our question

Does a country's policy record predict its maternal anaemia rate?

Sub-Saharan Africa, 2000 to 2023. Hold income and shared shocks fixed, then ask three things: where anaemia is high and which way it is moving, whether a country's policy record moves with its anaemia, and who beats what their context predicts.

Method

Each country compared with itself.

anaemiai,t=ai+gt+b · policyi,t−3+c · log GDPi,t+ei,t
aiCountry effect. Removes each country's level: geography, health system, malaria. What is left is the country against itself.
gtYear effect. Removes what hit all 49 at once: price spikes, COVID, changes in WHO's estimates.
bThe number we want. Within a country, how anaemia moves after more anaemia policy is in force three years earlier.
cIncome control, in logs, so a doubling counts the same at $800 as at $8,000.
Why the lag, and what the lead test checks
Policy three years earlier may predict today's anaemia. Policy three years later cannot; if it "does", governments are reacting to a trend.
Predict a country the model has never seen
Fit on 48 countries, predict the 49th's whole path, repeat 49 times. Compare the error with and without policy.

Sample: 994 country-years, 49 countries, 2003–2023. Standard errors clustered by country.

Finding 1

Where it is most concerning, and money is not the answer.

We scored 194 countries on four indicators: women of child bearing age anemia, pregnant women anemia, women's underweight proportion, and women's overweight proportion, looking at both the direction these indicators moved and the country's percentile rank compared to other countries. The five of most concern are Burundi, Jordan, Somalia, Lesotho and Lebanon. We focused down into Sub Saharan Africa where 3 of the 5 countries were located. Averaged over the 49 countries, anaemia fell from 44% to 37% between 2000 and 2015, then turned: higher in 2023 than in 2015 in 37 of 49 countries. Income alone explains under 5% of the differences between countries.

Finding 2

Both lines move together.

Policy stock rises and anaemia drifts down in nearly every country. A naive model calls that an effect. The test is to separate the policy from the shared trend.

Finding 2

The policy record does not move anaemia, measurably.

Inside a country, one more anaemia policy in force is followed three years later by −0.2 percentage points, indistinguishable from zero. The one row off zero is the lead: policies from the future "predict" today's anaemia, the fingerprint of governments reacting to a trend.

Finding 3

Who beats their context, and the paper trail cannot say.

Income, fertility, malaria, urbanisation, sanitation, sub-region and terrain explain 81% of the differences in levels. Nigeria, Namibia, Cameroon, Ghana and Liberia sit six to ten points below the level their context predicts. On the books, their policy record is not clearly different from the eight above them.

Recommendations

Measure delivery, not documents.

Data custodians · WHO, FAO

Record dates and coverage for every programme

Three in four programme records carry no date, and the coverage field is empty. Delivery data is the missing link.

Ministries · high-burden band

Report coverage at the point of delivery

Supplementation and fortification reach, not policy counts. Learn from the countries below their context: Nigeria, Namibia, Cameroon, Ghana, Liberia.

Funders and researchers

Anchor the estimates, pre-register the effect worth finding

More surveys that measure haemoglobin (33 of 49 countries have one in the window), and a smallest effect of interest set in advance, so a null reads as a bound, not an absence.

Next for us: subnational data, cost-of-diet linkage, sensitivity bounds for the event study, and schistosomiasis treatment coverage.

The last mile is measured in blood, not in documents.

Until delivery is recorded, nobody can say which policies work.
